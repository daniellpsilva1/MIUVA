1. What this topic is about

The European Health Data Space (EHDS) is a common set of EU rules that lets health data follow the patient for care — and be reused, under strict conditions, for research, innovation and policy.

On the map of health data recording and reuse, the EHDS sits at the junction of three activities: recording health data (topic 1), sharing it for care (primary use), and reusing it for research and policy (secondary use). It is one legal framework supporting two infrastructures: MyHealth@EU, which carries records across borders for a patient's own care, and HealthData@EU, which connects data holders and access bodies for governed reuse. Think of it as one rulebook with two roads: the care road and the reuse road both run on the same rules, but they lead to different places.

Jargon. The framework, the infrastructures, and the data holders are different layers. The framework sets the rules; MyHealth@EU and HealthData@EU are the technical pathways; hospitals, registries and laboratories are the data holders that keep the data.

What the EHDS is not

  • Not one big EU database. Data stay with the hospitals, registries and labs that hold them. The EHDS is the rulebook plus the connections between them.
  • Not a free-for-all. Reuse requires an application, a permit, and work inside a secure environment.
  • Not a quality guarantee. Rules can require quality labelling and stewardship; they cannot make incomplete or biased data fit a question.
  • Not a GDPR replacement. It adds health-specific rules on top of the GDPR; it does not lower its protections.

A staged timeline

The regulation exists as law but its duties arrive in stages. Published in the Official Journal on 5 March 2025, in force since 26 March 2025; implementing acts are due by 2027; most secondary-use rules apply from 2029; and the remaining, more sensitive categories — including genomic data — from 2031.

Common mistake. Saying secondary use "starts in 2031". Most secondary-use rules apply from 2029; 2031 extends the framework to more sensitive categories, it is not the beginning.

Who benefits

The Commission factsheet names five groups. Citizens gain control and continuity of care; health professionals gain access to relevant records; researchers and innovators gain facilitated access to data; policymakers and regulators gain relevant data for better policy; and industry gains growth opportunities — the factsheet puts the health data economy's potential at about €5.5 billion in EU savings over ten years from better access and exchange, €5.4 billion from better use of health data for research, innovation and policy-making, and 20–30% additional growth of the digital health market. These are the Commission's headline figures, intended benefits rather than guaranteed outcomes.

2. Key idea 1 — Primary and secondary use

Purpose decides the rules — not where the data travel. The same record can serve care and research, and the law treats those differently.

Primary use means using health data to care for the person it is about. Secondary use means reusing it to answer a question about many people — research, innovation, policy-making, regulatory work, public health. The record does not change; the purpose does.

Example. Sofia, 52, Amsterdam — a fictional example. Her GP records "glucose 7.0 mmol/L · fasting · 14 Jan". What happens to that one record?

ScenarioVerdictWhy
An emergency doctor in Spain opens her medication listPrimary useCare of Sofia herself, via MyHealth@EU
Sofia checks her own results on her phonePrimary useHer own access is a right under the EHDS
A university studies glucose trends in 50,000 patientsSecondary useA question about a population, via HealthData@EU
The health ministry decides where to open diabetes clinicsSecondary usePolicy-making counts too
An insurer wants her data to set her premiumNot permittedSecondary use has a fixed list of allowed purposes and explicit bans

Permitted and banned purposes

Secondary use runs on a fixed list of permitted purposes — scientific research, innovation, policy-making, regulatory activities, public health and related uses. Some uses are banned outright: decisions about an individual's insurance or employment, advertising, and anything harmful to the person.

Patient rights

Under the EHDS, people can access their own data, get a portable copy, and request corrections or restrictions. They can also opt out of secondary use in most cases — with a few public-interest exceptions, such as certain public-health and regulatory uses. Rectification is a right to request correction through the data holder, not to rewrite a clinical record yourself.

Common mistake. Thinking that crossing a border makes it research. A Spanish doctor treating a Dutch patient is still doing primary use — geography does not change the purpose.

3. Key idea 2 — Governed access and safeguards

Secondary use is governed access, not open access. Between a researcher's question and the data stand four steps.

  1. Find. A catalogue shows what data exist, where, and how good they are. Nothing is requested yet.
  2. Apply. The Health Data Access Body (HDAB) — a public gatekeeper every EU country must designate — checks the purpose and whether the data are really needed, then issues a permit.
  3. Analyse. Work happens in a secure processing environment — think library reading room: you can read and take notes, you can't take the book home.
  4. Take out. Only checked, non-personal results leave — statistics, a model. The data stay.

Jargon. Three roles stay distinct: the health data holder keeps the data (hospital, registry, lab); the HDAB is the public body that says yes or no; the health data user is the researcher or institution asking.

Pseudonymisation is not anonymisation

Example. A record that reads "ID: PAT-04821 · glucose 7.0 mmol/L · DOB 1972-03-14 · postcode 28013" has been pseudonymised: a code replaced the name, but a separately held key can link it back — it is still personal data, and other fields can re-identify the person anyway. An anonymised output looks different: "cohort n = 1,240 · mean fasting glucose 6.8 mmol/L" — nobody can be identified any more.

Pseudonymisation is a safeguard, not an erasure of identity. Pseudonymised access is not the default either — where anonymised data could answer the question, that is what should be used.

Five quick myth checks

"I can opt out of my data being reused"Fact (mostly)Opt-out applies to secondary use; a few public-interest exceptions remain.
"Any company can buy access to the data"MythOnly listed purposes, through a permit. Insurance pricing and advertising are banned outright.
"This already applies today"Myth, so farIn force since March 2025; most secondary-use rules from 2029; genomic and other sensitive categories from 2031.

4. Key idea 3 — Data quality

"The quality of data is always terrible until people start using it for a purpose." — Harald Wagener, Berlin Institute of Health, quoted in the report

Example. Three glucose records (fictional example). A: "7.0 mmol/L · fasting: yes" — usable: a number, a unit, and the context. B: "126 mg/dL · fasting: unknown" — the same value as A once you divide by 18, so the units are the easy fix; but nobody wrote down whether she was fasting, and you cannot recover that later. C: "slightly elevated", no number — a note, not a measurement; free text is still how most clinical data get entered, as the report notes for Spain.

Jargon. Interoperability is whether systems can exchange data and understand them the same way — units, codes. Data quality is whether the data actually fit the question you are asking — complete, correct, the right people. Interoperability makes data exchangeable; it does not make them correct.

Six dimensions of quality

DimensionQuestion it answers
AccuracyIs the value right?
CompletenessIs anything missing — and is the missingness random?
ConsistencyDo different records and systems agree?
ValidityDoes the variable measure what it claims?
UniquenessIs each thing recorded once, not duplicated?
TimelinessIs the data current enough for the question?

Missingness and equity

The report warns that the datasets the EHDS opens up underrepresent people living in low-income households, the homeless, immigrants, rural populations and young adults — and that the records of senior citizens, who stand to benefit most from reuse, are most often unstructured, free-text or paper. Biased data lead to biased policies and tools.

Who pays for quality?

Making data reusable costs real work. Estonia's experience suggests data holders shouldered as much as 80% of the burden of retrieving and making data available — while their main job is care.

Patient-generated data

Patient-recorded data can still be valuable: an inaccurate weight scale may be compensated by the fact that a measurement is provided on a daily basis. The trade-off is quality against usefulness.

What the framework does about it

The report points to the TEHDAS joint action — 25 countries building a common data-quality framework — and to quality labels for datasets. The regulation adopts the idea in Article 78, the data quality and utility label: a structured description of a dataset's documentation, quality and coverage. It is optional in general, but required for datasets collected with public funding.

Same rules, different starting points

5. How it fits together — closing the loop

Closing the loop means data recorded in care must travel through governed reuse to insight, and insight must be evaluated, adopted and paid for before it changes care — which produces new records.

Where the loop breaks

  1. Recording. Workflows are geared to primary use; forms are built for billing, not for questions, and paper and free text never make it in. The report describes hospitals whose systems are effectively immobilised during data extraction — a disproportionate burden on providers.
  2. Care. Silos that don't talk: interoperability is still the universal problem.
  3. Governed reuse. Slow contracts, high costs, unclear who pays.
  4. Insight. An association is not proof. The report cites an evaluation of three breast-cancer AI algorithms whose mammography scores varied with the equipment used — biased data give biased tools.
  5. Back to care. No reimbursement path, fear of AI, no time to change workflows. Adoption needs change management, not just evidence.

Cultural barriers

The roundtables report clinicians who see records as "their" data rather than the patient's, and patients who fear AI and losing contact with their doctor. Data-sharing culture is as much a barrier as technology.

Enablers the report proposes

  • "Enter once" structured forms that fit clinical work instead of adding to it.
  • Automated transfer from devices into the EHR and on to secondary use.
  • Insights fed back to clinicians, so contributing data visibly improves care.
  • Joint tenders and shared infrastructure to spread costs.
  • Reimbursement paths and value-based care models so that data-driven innovation can actually be paid for.
  • Active change management — training, workflow redesign, time.

The regulation adds one closing duty: significant findings about a person that emerge during secondary use must be returned so they can inform that person's care (Article 61(5)).

6. Our assessment

Right idea, uncertain delivery. Two conclusions are well supported; holding both at once is the point.

Convincing

  • The report doesn't just list problems — it proposes concrete fixes: a common data-quality framework (TEHDAS), quality labels for datasets, "enter once" forms that fit clinical work.
  • Common rules solve a real problem: today every country, even every hospital, has its own standards.
  • Purpose-based rules plus a gatekeeper is a credible answer to the trust question.

Challenging

  • Most of the extra work lands on clinicians and hospitals that are already short-staffed — and funding isn't guaranteed.
  • The evidence is expert opinion from 2023 roundtables, not measured results.
  • A common deadline doesn't create a common starting point.

What the report can and cannot establish

The report can establish that the barriers it names — paper records, free text, missing context, underrepresented groups, unclear costs — are real and recognised by the people who work with the data. It can establish that practical solutions exist and have precedent (Sweden's registries, France's SNDS, Estonia's experience).

It cannot establish that its recommendations will be affordable or effective at scale; they are informed proposals, not measured outcomes. And because it was written before the regulation was final, it describes the challenges that motivated the law — not the law as it now stands. Our one-liner: the framework is coherent; whether it works depends on who does the work and who pays.

7. Learning goals and exam practice

The three learning goals

  1. What the EHDS is. One EU framework, two infrastructures (MyHealth@EU for care, HealthData@EU for reuse); the data stay where they are. Who benefits: citizens, professionals, researchers, policymakers, industry.
  2. Primary versus secondary use. Purpose decides the rules: care of the person versus a question about many people. Secondary use means permit, secure environment, results only.
  3. The role of data quality. Interoperability makes data exchangeable; quality makes them fit for the question — and it is decided at the moment of recording.

Practice questions

1. A researcher gets a permit to study fasting glucose and cardiovascular events. The records arrive in two different units, fasting status is missing for many records, and some records contain only qualitative notes. The researcher harmonises the units and proceeds. What problems remain?

Model answer

Unit harmonisation fixes a syntactic problem: the values are now in a common form. It does not fix the missing fasting status, which is a contextual problem: a glucose value cannot be interpreted for a fasting-glucose study without knowing whether the patient was fasting. It does not fix the qualitative notes, which contain no numeric value and cannot be pooled without inventing data. The remaining problems are missingness (possibly non-random, biasing the sample), validity, and potential selection bias. The researcher should report the missingness, exclude or separately analyse the qualitative records rather than converting them, and state the limitations clearly. A larger sample does not repair these problems; it makes the bias more precise.

2. A doctor in Lisbon treats a patient from Warsaw and reads her record through MyHealth@EU. A week later, a research institute requests the same hospital's records for a cohort study. Both uses "cross a border" — why are they governed differently?

Model answer

Because purpose, not geography, defines the use. The doctor's read is primary use — care of the individual — carried by the primary-use infrastructure under the patient's own rights. The institute's request is secondary use — a question about a population — which requires a permitted purpose, an application to the health data access body, a permit, and analysis in a secure environment where only non-personal results leave. The same records are involved; the rules differ because the purposes differ.

3. A colleague says: "Once the record is pseudonymised, the patient is anonymous, so the safeguards are just paperwork." What is wrong with this claim?

Model answer

Two things. Pseudonymisation replaces identifiers with a code, but a separately held key can re-link it — the data remain personal data, so the GDPR and EHDS safeguards still apply; only anonymisation makes a person no longer identifiable. And removing a name may not even reach pseudonymisation in practice, because other fields — date of birth, postcode, rare diagnoses — can re-identify the person.

The friend test

Turn to your neighbour: explain the EHDS in three sentences. Sixty seconds is enough. If you cannot do it, reread chapters 1 and 2.

8. Discussion questions

  1. Trust question. Would you trust your health data to be shared and used across Europe? Why or why not? Optional follow-up: what would have to be true for the safeguards to feel credible to you?
  2. Responsibility question. Should responsibility for high-quality data rest mainly on professionals, or be shared across institutions, governments and the EU? Optional follow-up: what would make good recording the easy option rather than extra work?

How to run them: keep both non-personal — ask what would make sharing feel safer, not whether anyone has a diagnosis. Invite one voice on each side: "clinicians first" against "the system has to make it easy". Neither question has a required answer.

10. Glossary and sources

Glossary

Anonymisation
Processing that makes individuals no longer identifiable; anonymised data are not personal data.
Data quality
Whether data fit the question being asked — accurate, complete, consistent, valid, unique, timely.
Data quality and utility label
Article 78's structured description of a dataset's documentation, quality and coverage; optional except for publicly funded datasets.
Health Data Access Body (HDAB)
The public gatekeeper each Member State designates to assess applications and issue permits for secondary use.
Health data holder
The entity that holds the data — hospital, registry, laboratory — and prepares it for access.
HealthData@EU
The secondary-use infrastructure connecting HDABs and data holders across the EU.
Interoperability
The ability of systems to exchange data and understand them the same way — units and codes.
MyHealth@EU
The primary-use infrastructure for cross-border exchange of records for care.
Primary use
Using health data to care for the person the data are about.
Pseudonymisation
Replacing identifiers with a code that a separately held key can re-link; the data remain personal data.
Secondary use
Reusing health data to answer a question about many people — research, innovation, policy, public health.
Secure processing environment
The locked reading room where permitted analysis happens; only checked, non-personal results leave.
