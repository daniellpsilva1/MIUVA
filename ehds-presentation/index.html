University seminar

The European Health Data Space

What changes when a health record can travel — for care, and then for learning.

EIT Health Think Tank (2024), chapters 4–5 · Regulation (EU) 2025/327

Open with a concrete change: a record that can move across borders changes what care and research are possible. The EHDS is a framework, not a database. It joins primary and secondary use under common rules. Do not list articles here; make the audience curious about how it works. Spend about 45 seconds. Transition: the central distinction is purpose.

Reg. (EU) 2025/327, Arts. 5–9, 53 · Colleagues' notes

The central distinction: the same record can serve care and research, and purpose determines the rules. A doctor abroad treating Sofia is primary use — the fact that the data crossed a border does not turn care into research. The same record later used to study a cohort is secondary use. The record does not change; the purpose does. Reveal the classification with a click or the space bar. Keep the corrective explanation short: purpose, not geography, defines the use. About 1 minute 30 seconds. Transition: if secondary use is different, how does a researcher actually get access?

The data do not leave. Only reviewed, non-personal results do.

Reg. (EU) 2025/327, Arts. 66–73 · Teaching illustration, not a permit service

Secondary use is governed access, not open access. Step through the pathway: question and catalogue, application, HDAB assessment, permit, secure environment, reviewed non-personal output. Emphasise the secure-environment boundary: the data themselves do not leave; only reviewed non-personal outputs do. This is the most misunderstood point. The optional research/marketing comparison shows "potentially eligible; assessment required", not automatic approval. Label the whole scene as a teaching illustration. About 1 minute 45 seconds. Reg. (EU) 2025/327, Arts. 66–73 · Colleagues' notes

Correct the common conflation of pseudonymisation with anonymisation. Pseudonymisation replaces identifiers with codes but allows re-linking through a separately held key — it is still personal data. Anonymisation makes individuals no longer identifiable. Removing a name alone does neither: other fields like date of birth and postcode can re-identify. Do not simulate anonymisation as a cosmetic toggle; the side-by-side comparison makes the distinction concrete. About 1 minute 15 seconds. EIT Health (2024), ch. 4 · Fictional teaching example

The key teaching example. Three records: A (7.0 mmol/L, fasting), B (126 mg/dL, fasting unknown), C (qualitative note). Step 1: the units are approximately equivalent — conversion is a multiplication by about 18. Step 2: harmonising units does not recover B's missing fasting status, and C has no number to convert. Step 3: harmonisation fixes syntax, not context, missingness, or bias. Let the example breathe; the missing-context point is the key. No medical diagnosis is implied. About 2 minutes. Transition: so the data are exchangeable — but are they fit for the question?

Usable for which question — and for whom?

Exchange
Can the system send and receive the data?
Meaning
Do both sides understand the same code the same way?
Validity
Does the variable measure what it claims?
Completeness
Is the data missing — and is the missingness random?
Provenance
Where did the value come from, and what has happened to it?
Coverage
Whose data are here — and whose are not?

Interoperability makes data exchangeable. It does not make them correct, complete, or representative. Data quality is fitness for a specific question.

EIT Health (2024), ch. 4 · Reg. (EU) 2025/327, Art. 78

Distinguish interoperability from quality, and quality from fitness for a specific question. The passport contrasts exchange and meaning — interoperability — with validity, completeness, provenance, and coverage — quality. Counting recorded tests is not the same as studying treatment effectiveness. Harmonisation does not remove selection bias. Do not over-explain FAIR; the passport makes the point. EIT Health (2024), chs. 4–5 · Colleagues' notes

Making records reusable is work, and most of it falls on healthcare organisations. Structured entry, recording once, metadata, funded support, validated text extraction. Clinical care remains the priority. The Ireland/Spain contrast is dated to 2023 consultations — not a permanent ranking. The point is unequal starting points under common deadlines. Name the workload; do not dismiss it as logistics. About 1 minute 30 seconds. Transition: even if the data are ready and the research is done, what has to happen before it changes care?

What has to happen before research changes care?

Research
A permitted analysis finds an association.
→
Evaluation
Does it replicate? Does the intervention work when tried?
→
Adoption
Training, guidance, reimbursement, workflow change.
→
Monitoring
Does it work in practice? What harms appear?
↺ New data feed back into the next cycle

A permitted analysis is not a validated intervention.

EIT Health (2024), ch. 5 · Closing the loop · Colleagues' notes

The loop: research, evaluation, adoption, monitoring, and back. Each step can fail. An association is not a causal claim; more data does not by itself produce causal evidence. Evaluation turns a finding into a candidate intervention. Adoption requires training and reimbursement. Monitoring closes the loop back to data. Emphasise the evaluation/adoption gap — this is where the loop most often breaks. A permitted analysis is not a validated intervention. About 1 minute 45 seconds. Transition: step back — what should we conclude?

A coherent framework. Unequal starting points.

Strength

The EHDS joins data collection and benefit under common rules — a path from care data to research data more coherent than the patchwork it replaces.

Limitation

Expert recommendations do not prove affordable, equitable delivery. Common deadlines apply to countries at very different stages.

Hold both conclusions at once. The framework is promising and its implementation is uncertain.
