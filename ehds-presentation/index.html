03 · Distinction · 2 min

Purpose — not profession, not geography

Primary and secondary use are defined by why the data is processed, not by who holds it or where it travels.

Primary use

Care of this person

Sofia’s clinician checks her results to treat her.

“What does this patient need?”

Secondary use

Learning beyond this case

Researchers study outcomes across many patients.

“What can we learn beyond this individual?”

The same data can support different purposes, under different conditions.
Check: If Sofia’s records are sent to another country so a doctor can treat her, is that primary or secondary use? Primary. Purpose, not location.
Legal definitions, and why pseudonymisation is not anonymisation ▶

Primary use: Processing of personal electronic health data for the provision of health services to assess, maintain or restore the state of health of the natural person to whom that data relates — including prescription, dispensation, and relevant social security or reimbursement services.

Secondary use: Processing of electronic health data for purposes other than the diagnosis, treatment and care of the patient — including research, innovation, public health, policymaking, and regulatory activities.

Health data access bodyA designated body set up by EU Member States to provide access to electronic health data to third parties for secondary use in a secure way, building on the Data Governance Act. assesses and manages access for secondary use.

Pseudonymisation ≠ anonymisation. Identifiers are replaced and the key is kept separately. Pseudonymised data remains personal data.

EIT Health (2024), ch. 5, pp. 84–89; EHDS Regulation definitions.

Why records fall short

  • Different formats, codes and measurement conventions
  • Missing information and unstructured free text
  • Missing context about how data was collected
  • Unequal representation of patients and settings

National snapshots

Austria — lack of standards▶

Nurses lack access to the national EHR; many work in unstructured silos such as basic spreadsheets. Suboptimal reporting in national registries (SARI, cancer, death statistics).

Germany — siloed care▶

Ranks low on integrated care among OECD countries. Paper documentation in nursing and home care keeps entire categories of health data inaccessible for research.

Ireland & Spain — paper and free text▶

Ireland’s reliance on paper records made data quality a low priority. In Spain, only a small percentage of EMR input fields are filled in routine practice; most data is entered as free text.

Data equity: People who rarely access healthcare — low-income households, the homeless, immigrants, rural populations, young adults — may be poorly represented. Adding more records from well-represented groups does not automatically solve that bias. In Estonia, data holders shouldered up to 80% of the burden of making data reusable.
More data does not automatically mean better evidence.

EIT Health (2024), ch. 4, pp. 74–77: fragmentation, standardisation, costs and underrepresentation.

FAIR — findable, accessible, interoperable, reusable ▶

Findable — discoverable through standardised identifiers.

A

Accessible — available under defined conditions.

Interoperable — common languages and formats.

Reusable — well described for new purposes.

The EHDS legislation builds on FAIR and data equity to define EU-wide standards for quality and utility.

Metadata, traceability, ontology, TEHDAS ▶

MetadataInformation describing a dataset, including variables, units, collection methods and coverage. — so users can judge suitability.

TraceabilityThe ability to follow data's origins and processing history across primary and secondary use systems. — origins and processing history.

Data ontologyA standardised vocabulary that enables sharing of information between disparate systems within the same domain — e.g., terms for medical specialties or diseases. — shared vocabulary.

TEHDASJoint Action Towards the European Health Data Space — an EU-funded project involving 25 countries that developed a data quality framework for secondary use. — a harmonised quality framework for trustworthy secondary use.

Intelligent collection▶

Well-designed EHR forms aligned with clinical practice, entering information once where possible, and carefully implemented capture from devices can reduce burden while improving quality.

NLP for legacy free text▶

Natural language processing can help standardise decades of historical free-text records that would otherwise remain inaccessible for secondary use.

Qualification: Automation can help; it does not guarantee accuracy. Poorly designed automation spreads errors more efficiently.
Value-based healthcare as an enabler ▶

Value-based healthcare pays for results rather than services, incentivising keeping people healthy over treating the sick. That aligns with the predictive and preventive models the EHDS is expected to enable.

EIT Health (2024), ch. 5, pp. 84–91; supporting context in ch. 4, pp. 76 and 80–82.

Click a stage · clockwise: a continuous learning cycle
Research result ≠ validated intervention ≠ routine clinical adoption.
Incidental findings▶

A standardised path is needed for secondary users to recontact patients via their healthcare teams — to request complementary information or report incidental findings, defining how, where, and to whom the information must be provided.

Adoption and reimbursement▶

Examples: Catalonia’s BIOCAT fast-track for digital health technologies; Austria’s “digital health pathway”. Value-based models pay for results, incentivising prevention.

08 · Critique · 1.5 min

Convincing — and unfinished

One strength and one limitation are enough. Keep the critique proportionate.

What holds

The argument is joined-up

  • Connects quality at collection with the value of reuse.
  • Takes clinical workload and the return of benefit seriously.
  • Does not treat data sharing as an end in itself.
What remains open

Recommendations are not evidence

  • Expert advice is not proof of effectiveness.
  • Fair funding and practical adoption remain hard.
  • Who pays to make data reusable, when benefits are shared widely?
What the method can and cannot establish ▶

The report draws on expert roundtables and interviews across 10 countries during 2022–2023. Useful for naming barriers and possible solutions; it does not establish which solutions will work best or what they will cost.

Read the legal discussion historically: it reflects consultations before the Regulation entered into force in March 2025.

Keep the critique proportionate: Evaluate it as an implementation report. Do not fault it for failing to be a clinical trial. Separate the authors’ recommendations from your group’s assessment.

10 · Discussion · 2 min

Two questions, if time allows

Show both. Discuss one if the clock is tight.

01 Is more data always better? ▶

Would you prefer a very large hospital dataset or a smaller dataset that better represents your target population? What would you need to know before deciding?

Facilitation: Start with the research question and target population. Explore missing information, selection bias, quality and comparability. Avoid declaring either “larger” or “smaller” automatically best.
02 Who should do — and fund — the extra work? ▶

Should clinicians record additional information mainly for future research? Under what conditions?

Facilitation: Keep patient-care priorities central. Look for usable workflows, no duplicate work, staff and funding. Ask what benefits would make the extra effort worthwhile.
The challenge is not simply collecting more data. It is collecting and reusing the right data responsibly, in ways that produce meaningful benefits.

Appendix · FAQ

If they ask

Short answers. Rehearse them out loud once.

Is the EHDS one central European database?▶

No. It is a common framework involving rules, institutions and infrastructures. One central database is a misleading description.

Is secondary use always research?▶

No. It also includes public health, policymaking, innovation and regulatory activities, within the applicable rules.

Does sending records abroad make it secondary use?▶

No. If the purpose is treating the individual, it remains primary use.

Does standardisation guarantee good data?▶

No. Standardised records can still contain errors, missing information or unrepresentative populations.

Can imperfect data still be useful?▶

Yes. Suitability depends on the question and whether the limitations can be handled. Imperfect does not mean worthless.

Why not just clean the data later?▶

Cleaning helps, but cannot reliably recover information never recorded or automatically correct missing populations. It also costs time and resources.

Are pseudonymised data anonymous?▶

No. Pseudonymised data remains personal data; anonymisation is different.

Does the EHDS remove GDPR protections?▶

No. The EHDS builds on and complements existing data-protection law.

Does secondary use automatically improve care?▶

No. Findings need evaluation, adoption and monitoring. That is why closing the loop is central.

Is the EHDS already fully implemented?▶

No. It entered into force in March 2025; major provisions apply progressively, notably in 2029 and 2031.

Appendix · Practice

Explain the relationship

Practice, not a prediction of the actual exam. Do not just list terms.

Question: Explain how data quality affects the relationship between primary and secondary use in the European Health Data Space.
Model answer▶

Primary use involves using health data to provide care to the individual, while secondary use includes purposes such as research and policymaking. Data recorded during care can support secondary use, but it was not necessarily collected with those later purposes in mind.

Differences in coding, units, completeness and population coverage can make records difficult to combine or produce misleading findings. Interoperability helps systems exchange and interpret information, while metadata and traceability help users understand its context and limitations. Data quality must therefore be assessed in relation to the intended question.

Reusable data should be supported through appropriate recording practices, standards and resources without unnecessarily burdening clinical care. For secondary use to improve healthcare, its findings must then be evaluated and incorporated into practice, completing the feedback loop.

Why this works: it defines the two purposes, explains why care data may fail a later task, links interoperability with quality and representation, and returns evaluated findings to care.

What we should leave with

The EHDS is not just about moving health data. It is about making that data usable for the right purpose — and turning what we learn into better care.
